# Neighbours Club — Go-to-Market & Competitive Thesis

**Status:** Founder strategy doc. Anchors how we answer "how do you compete with
the incumbents" and what we must prove before scaling.
**Companion to:** docs/product-thesis.md (the WHY) — this is the HOW-we-win.

---

## 0. The question this answers

"How do you compete with the massive marketing budgets and existing user base of
incumbents like Uber Eats or Nextdoor? They have everyone already."

Most mission-driven local plays die on this question — not because the mission is
wrong, but because they have no real answer for distribution. This is our answer.

---

## 1. Reframe the premise: local density, not national reach

"They have everyone already" is true nationally and false locally — and local is
the only board we play on.

- Uber Eats has *transactions* in Kanata. It does not have *Kanata*. Nobody feels
  loyalty to Uber Eats; they tolerate it.
- Nextdoor has presence but is widely experienced as a toxic, anxiety-driven feed
  people use and resent.
- The incumbents' user base is **wide and shallow**. We win by being **narrow and
  deep**: a neighbourhood densely engaged beats national reach with thin mindshare.

Local commerce is a **density game, not a reach game.** 60% of one Kanata
neighbourhood genuinely engaged beats 100% reach at 2% mindshare. We are not
trying to win everyone. We are trying to win a neighbourhood, completely.

---

## 2. The two structural advantages incumbents cannot copy

We don't outspend them. We out-*structure* them, in two ways their own business
model forbids them from matching.

### 2.1 Merchants are our distribution, and their incentives align with us
Every Kanata restaurant currently hands 15-30% to a platform that commoditizes
them. We offer materially better economics. A merchant keeping their margin has a
direct financial reason to drive *their own* customers to our platform — they will
tell customers to order through us.

That is distribution we don't pay for, motivated by the same extraction we're
built against. **The incumbents' business model is our acquisition channel** — and
they cannot respond by treating merchants better without breaking their own unit
economics.

### 2.2 A structural trust position they cannot occupy
Our moat is not a feature; it's a promise the incumbents literally cannot make:
"we will not manipulate you." They can't say it credibly because their P&L depends
on manipulation (dark patterns, engagement-bait, opaque pricing).

In a tight, tech-literate community that talks to itself, "the platform that's
actually on our side and keeps our money here" spreads through word-of-mouth and
local institutions (neighbourhood associations, the merchants themselves) — exactly
the channels national ad budgets penetrate poorly. We don't buy attention; we earn
standing.

---

## 3. The cold-start answer: saturate one node

None of the above matters if we can't get the FIRST node to critical density.
This is a cold-start problem, and the answer is NOT to boil the ocean or "compete
with Uber Eats."

We saturate ONE Kanata neighbourhood: a defined set of merchants, the local
association, enough residents that it feels alive. We prove the loop —
money stays local → merchants advocate → residents trust it → density compounds —
in one place, then replicate neighbourhood by neighbourhood.

The incumbents' marketing budget is irrelevant to whether we can make ONE
neighbourhood work. One working neighbourhood is the only proof that matters
before we scale. (See: Node Liquidity & Seeding Playbook.)

---

## 4. The one-sentence version (for an investor)

"We don't compete with their budget — we make it irrelevant. They win national
reach; we win neighbourhood density, using the merchants they extract from as our
distribution and a trust position they structurally can't occupy. We're not trying
to beat Uber Eats everywhere; we're proving one Kanata neighbourhood, where their
budget doesn't help them and their model works against them."

---

## 5. The three gaps to close BEFORE leaning on this pitch

A sharp investor will follow up on exactly these. Knowing the soft spots is the
prep list. Do not pitch hard on this thesis until these are closed.

### 5.1 The merchant-economics claim must be REAL numbers (load-bearing)
"Better economics" only works with a concrete, defensible take-rate: what we
charge, that it's sustainably below Uber Eats, AND that *we* survive on it.
Take-rate policy is still an OPEN DECISION in our roadmap (Tier 0). This is where
the pitch currently outruns the model. Close this first — it's the foundation of
advantage 2.1.

### 5.2 The cold-start needs a concrete seeding plan
Which neighbourhood, which merchants, how we get the first ~100 engaged residents.
The Node Liquidity & Seeding Playbook is the artifact that answers this; we must be
able to speak to it crisply, not in principle.

### 5.3 The cold-start runs on VALUE, not on the mission
"Trust compounds" is true but slow, and investors know it. Fair pushback: "trust is
a great moat once you have it — what gets the first thousand users *before* the
trust exists?" The answer is the merchant-driven distribution (2.1) and tangible
resident value (cheaper, useful local info, not annoying) — the *acquisition pitch*,
not the *north-star problem*.

KEY DISTINCTION (from product-thesis.md): the principles are the MOAT, but the
cold-start runs on tangible merchant incentives and resident value. **Lead
marketing and the cold-start with the surface value; build the product on the
depth. Do not pitch the cold-start on the mission.**

---

## 6. The compass test for future features

Every future feature should pass: does this keep value/trust local and resist
extraction, or does it drift toward the thing we define ourselves against? The
Notes editorial firewall passes. Countdown-timer demurrage failed exactly because
it violated this. This thesis is the test, not just the pitch.
